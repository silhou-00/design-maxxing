The grid is an argument, not a container

By A. Reyes · 14 min · Photographs by M. Okafor

Nine words in the deck — the only pack here that raises the engine's eight-word ceiling, because a magazine deck is a sentence rather than a label, and the body runs at seventeen pixels on a sixty-four character measure.

01 — The ratio

Contrast is the whole trick

A forty-six pixel display against fourteen pixel body, set on warm paper rather than white. Match the sizes and you have a blog.

Type carries the voice; the image only sets the room.

There is no card here and there will not be one. Editorial content sits on the page, separated by a column rule and by space, exactly as it does in print — which is why the ban on cards is load-bearing rather than stylistic.

Scrollytelling is not an effect bolted onto this style. The sticky image with text moving beside it is literally a spread, which is why it is the one technique rated core here and why snap is banned: snapping fights reading.

Everything above has been one column of continuous prose at a fixed measure. That is the test — if the layout cannot hold a long argument, it is not editorial.

The column has to be substantially taller than the figure beside it, or the pin lasts a second and the technique reads as a glitch. That is a composition requirement, not a CSS one: scrollytelling needs enough text to scroll past.

A pin that does not hold is worse than no pin.

Warm paper rather than white, ink at #141414 rather than pure black, and a serif set at seventeen pixels on a 1.6 leading. None of that is decoration — it is the configuration that makes long-form text comfortable to read.

The kicker above each section does the work an eyebrow does elsewhere, and the pull quote breaks the column without breaking the measure. Both are print devices, and both survive the move to a screen unchanged.

When the figure finally releases, it should be because the argument has finished — not because the container ran out.
